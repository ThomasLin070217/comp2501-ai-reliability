# GSM-Plus v3 results card — for the English report

**Study question.** On selected arithmetic word problems, does asking MiniMax to check its own first answer lower the wrong-answer rate, and does showing it a second model's independent answer lower it further? The second model was DeepSeek. Both follow-ups began independently from the **same MiniMax initial conversation**; they were parallel branches, not a sequence of revisions.

**Protocol and denominator.** We froze 500 GSM-Plus v3 questions, obtained one independent initial answer from each model per question, then ran one neutral MiniMax self-check and one natural MiniMax cross-check on each of 496 questions whose two initial answers were usable. All 992 follow-up tasks returned complete responses. One question produced `#` in both follow-ups and was unscorable, leaving **495 common scorable question pairs** for the three-way comparison. An unscorable response is not an active refusal. The numerator below includes only genuinely wrong final answers; a correct answer or explicit abstention is nonwrong.

| MiniMax condition on the same 495 questions | Wrong | Wrong-answer rate |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Initial answer | 17/495 | 3.43% |
| Neutral self-check | 9/495 | 1.82% |
| Natural cross-check after seeing DeepSeek's actual independent reply | 4/495 | 0.81% |

The question-level paired bootstrap (5,000 resamples, fixed seed) estimates self-check minus initial at **−1.62 percentage points** (95% interval **−3.03 to −0.40**), natural cross-check minus initial at **−2.63 points** (**−4.24 to −1.21**), and natural cross-check minus self-check at **−1.01 points** (**−2.22 to 0.00**). Self-check eliminated or corrected 10 initial errors and introduced two new errors; natural cross-check eliminated or corrected 14 and introduced one. In the direct parallel-branch comparison, natural cross-check had seven cases where self was wrong and natural was nonwrong, and two with the reverse pattern. Thus both checks reduced wrong answers in this selected set, while a stable advantage of natural cross-check over self-check is **not established** by this interval.

**Can an actually wrong peer corrupt a correct answer?** Among the 495 common questions, the MiniMax initial answer was correct and the independent DeepSeek answer wrong in only **two** cases. On one, DeepSeek counted trees as flowers; the natural cross-check rejected that error and remained correct. On the other, the natural cross-check adopted DeepSeek's $900 commission answer rather than the frozen key's $450, but the original commission wording permits another plausible rate interpretation. That case is not clean proof that false peer advice caused a genuinely wrong final answer. The separate frozen 50-question **scripted wrong-peer** test produced **0/50 wrong final answers and 0/50 false-target adoptions**; the same 50 questions' neutral self-checks were also **0/50 wrong**. These selected, initially correct questions do not show an induced error under that particular prompt, but cannot establish zero risk elsewhere. The researcher-authored false advice is not a real DeepSeek reply.

**Grading audit and limits.** An R-generated queue required full-text review of 255/992 follow-up replies: 133 last-number/key discordances, two nonnumeric replies, and a predetermined 120-response screen-positive audit (60 per branch). Every required reply was reviewed. The positive-screen audit found **120/120 correct**, while 737 remaining positive-screen replies were graded by the frozen screen without individual full-text review. Six reviewed replies had answer/explanation conflicts, including four correct final answers and two wrong final answers. The questions were selected and baseline MiniMax error was low, so these results do not estimate performance across all mathematics or ordinary AI use. The post-hoc exclusion of four wording-sensitive questions yields self 6/491 wrong and natural 3/491 wrong, with a natural-minus-self interval **−1.63 to +0.20 points**; this does not revise the primary frozen-key result.
