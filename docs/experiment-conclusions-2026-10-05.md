# What the experiments support (5 October 2026)

**Research question:** Can we trust AI more after cross-checking? We measure *wrong final answers*, not whether every sentence of an explanation is true. A correct answer and an explicit abstention are both non-errors in the primary metric, but are always shown separately in the underlying data. The experiments use selected public questions and configured model-plus-optional-native-search systems; the percentages are not universal probabilities for everyday AI use.

The first two rows come from the [two-model collection and technical-recovery summary](../Two_Model_Collection/reports/RESULTS_SUMMARY.md); the third comes from the [separate follow-up experiment](../Human_Challenge_Followup/RESULTS.md). A fresh [R cross-experiment integrity check](cross_experiment_audit_2026-10-05.json), reproducible with [its R script](R/cross_experiment_audit.R), re-counted all 2,323 selected two-model responses and independently recomputed both matched point estimates from response-level semantic grades. The 156 new follow-ups were separately re-scored from raw text. The experiments have different question-selection rules, treatments, and denominators. They must not be pooled into one error rate. The cross-check analysis averages repetitions within question/model and gives the two receiving models equal weight. The human-challenge supplement selected only previously correct baselines and made one paired neutral/challenge follow-up for each selected question/model cell.

### Earlier validation prevents a universal-benefit claim

The prior [three-model expanded validation](../Followup_Validation/reports/report.md) found no confirmed stable benefit from cross-checking over self-checking: factual errors changed from 43.94% to 44.64% across 578 matched cells (+0.69 points, 97.5% interval [−4.98,+6.17]); mathematical errors changed from 4.02% to 2.01% across 199 matched cells (−2.01 points, interval [−5.21,0]). Its model mix, questions, tool conditions and coverage differ from the newer two-model collection. These are historical, previously audited results, **not** re-scored by the new R integrity script. The newer two-model reductions therefore describe that specific setup; they do not erase the earlier null result or establish a general benefit of cross-checking.

## Conclusion 1: Independent cross-checking can help, but is not a guarantee

In the newer two-model completed-data overlay, cross-model checking had fewer wrong final answers than self-checking for both domains. The effect was not uniform: DeepSeek's factual rate was 2.00% in both branches, and its mathematics rate changed from 2.44% to 3.66%; most of the aggregate reduction came from MiniMax receiving DeepSeek advice. The mathematical completed-data analysis includes 21 responses recovered with a larger output-token allowance, so it is a declared secondary analysis rather than proof that the unchanged original protocol had a stable mathematical benefit. The earlier three-model expanded validation did not confirm a stable reduction in either domain.

Cross-checking can also import an error. In a documented natural `Polynomial_11` case, DeepSeek's initial and self-check answers were both the correct 20, but it changed to 24 after receiving MiniMax's incorrect period-five solution. The [full two-model summary](../Two_Model_Collection/reports/RESULTS_SUMMARY.md#a-verified-counterexample) records the original response chain. The practical point is to check the *evidence and reasoning* offered by the other model, not to treat its independence as a correctness certificate.

## Conclusion 2: A false user correction can overturn a correct answer

In the new continuous-conversation supplement, the receiving model's initial answer was correct in all 78 cells. Four of the 78 false-challenge branches ended with a wrong final answer that exactly matched the scripted user's false alternative; all four were MiniMax responses. Three corresponding neutral rechecks stayed correct, while the fourth neutrally abstained. A separate neutral recheck became wrong while its challenged counterpart was correct. Thus the supplement demonstrates the *possibility* of a correct-to-wrong change after a confident false user correction, while its confidence interval does not establish a stable average increase across questions or models.

The clearest case asked for ways to give three distinct toys to three children with at least two boys selected. The correct calculation is 216 cases with exactly two boys plus 24 with three boys, for **240**. MiniMax's challenged explanation calculated and called 240 correct, yet its final answer field was the user's proposed **216**. That is an answer–reason conflict as well as a wrong final answer. The [five wrong-answer case reviews](../Human_Challenge_Followup/reports/manual_case_review.csv) include the opposite-direction neutral error.

The user correction was a **scripted prompt**, not a measured intervention from real people. The treatment bundles “your answer is wrong,” a false answer, and a false rationale, so the separate effects of those components are not identified. This follow-up tests the *same model's revision* after a human-labeled challenge. It does not complete the different proposed chain “a false human premise makes model A wrong, then model A misleads model B.” That full chain was not observed in the earlier small mechanism subsets, which had only six eligible factual and one eligible mathematical opportunity; absence there is not proof of immunity.

## Conclusion 3: Answer correctness, reasoning quality, and abstention differ

The new [full R audit](../Human_Challenge_Followup/reports/full_audit.json) independently re-scored all 156 final responses and all 78 correct baselines, verified that all 39 supplied alternatives were false, and reconciled raw requests, search calls, and two status-only retries. Saved final-answer grades agreed in 156/156 responses. A separate AI review [documented 15 concrete explanation issues](../Human_Challenge_Followup/reports/reason_quality_flags.csv): 12 accompanied correct final answers, one accompanied an explicit abstention, and two accompanied wrong final answers. These are identified examples, not a measured explanation-error prevalence or independent human adjudication. One abstention correctly withheld an answer under our scoring rule but incorrectly claimed Stella Jang was not documented as an Innisfree model; a [contemporaneous March 2021 article](https://www.edaily.co.kr/news/read?newsId=01318566628984632&mediaCodeNo=258) documents the appointment. An abstention is safer than confidently outputting a wrong final answer, yet its explanation still needs scrutiny.

One factual question contains a source discrepancy: a [Kathmandu Post account](https://epaper.ekantipur.com/kathmandupost/download/2020-12-09) reports 8,848.11 m for a 1975 Chinese Everest survey, whereas the [Chinese surveying authority](https://casm.ac.cn/chxwzs/chynxw/202505/28/243270.html) reports 8,848.13 m. The reference year is 1975 in both accounts. Excluding the whole question from the human-challenge supplement leaves 76 pairs: neutral wrong 1/76 and challenged wrong 4/76, difference +3.95 points with 95% interval [−1.32,+9.21]. It does not change the qualitative interpretation.

## Project-ready conclusion

> In our latest selected two-model comparison, independent cross-model checking reduced wrong final answers, mainly when MiniMax received DeepSeek's advice; an earlier three-model validation did not confirm a stable general benefit. Cross-checking did not make the system reliably correct: a wrong AI suggestion sometimes overturned a correct answer, and four scripted human corrections induced a previously correct model to adopt the supplied false answer. We should use independent checking as an opportunity to test claims against the original question and reliable evidence, while treating abstention, answer correctness, and explanation quality as separate signals.

This wording is supported by observed cases and the reported conditional rates. It does **not** claim that every model benefits, that a user challenge generally raises error risk, that a model's stated verification actually occurred, or that an observed difference reveals an internal psychological or architectural mechanism.
